What Belongs in a Commercial Cleaning Scope of Work

The elements every commercial cleaning scope of work should include, and why a quote without a scope is not a quote.

A commercial cleaning contract without a written scope of work is a promise without a standard. When something goes wrong—and eventually something goes wrong in every cleaning relationship—the scope of work is the document that determines whether the contractor is accountable or whether the dispute devolves into a disagreement about what "commercial cleaning" means.

This guide describes what a complete scope of work includes and what should concern you if it is missing.

Zone Definitions

A complete scope identifies the building by address and lists every zone to be cleaned: offices by number or name, restrooms by location and stall count, kitchens, conference rooms, reception areas, corridors, and any excluded areas. If a space is not named in the scope, it is not included in the service. A scope that says "all common areas" without naming them is a scope that will be interpreted narrowly by the contractor when it is convenient.

Task Definitions by Zone

For each zone, the scope should list the specific tasks, not just the general service category. "Vacuuming" is a task. "Cleaning" is not. A complete zone entry might read: vacuum all carpeted surfaces, empty and reline waste receptacles, wipe conference table and chairs with a damp cloth, clean whiteboard if marked, and spot-clean glass wall panels. This level of specificity is what allows a supervisor to verify the service and what allows you to report a service failure precisely.

Frequency Per Task

Not all tasks occur at every service visit. Cleaning the insides of microwaves might happen weekly. Dusting high surfaces might happen monthly. Stripping and waxing VCT floors happens quarterly. The scope should state the frequency for every task. A task without a stated frequency defaults to "whenever the crew decides to do it," which is not a standard.

Cleaning Schedule

The scope should specify the service days (Monday through Friday, Monday/Wednesday/Friday, etc.), the earliest entry time, and the latest required completion time. For buildings with alarm systems, the scope should reference the alarm deactivation procedure and document who holds the code. For key-card access buildings, the scope should confirm whether the crew is issued cards or contacts a security desk.

Crew Assignment

The scope should name the assigned crew lead and provide direct contact information. If a crew change occurs during the contract term, you should receive written notice and the opportunity to meet the replacement before the first service. A scope that does not address crew assignment is a scope from a dispatcher-model contractor.

Service Failure Response

The scope should define what constitutes a service failure, the reporting process, and the response commitment. A reasonable standard is: service failures reported before noon are addressed by end of business the same day; failures reported after noon are addressed by noon the following business day. Contractors who resist putting a response commitment in writing are contractors who plan to manage your complaints as exceptions rather than as a defined process.

Products and Equipment

The scope should confirm that all cleaning products, equipment, and supplies are provided by the contractor and included in the contract price. If you have specific product requirements—fragrance-free disinfectants, green chemistry preferences, or products required by a lease or compliance standard—those requirements belong in the scope.
